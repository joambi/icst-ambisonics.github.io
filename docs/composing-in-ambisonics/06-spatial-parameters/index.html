Spatial Parameters as Compositional Material

The concept of Spatial Counterpoint developed in the preceding section understands space as a relational and form-generating principle of musical organization. For this claim to extend beyond the fleeting disposition of a particular performance situation, a format is required that makes spatial relations storable, editable, and transferable across different playback conditions. Within Ambisonics, the B-format fulfills this function: it makes it possible to conceive spatial constellations as abstract structures and to fix them independently of any concrete playback system. In this sense, 3D audio is not treated as a secondary means of sound distribution, but as a formative element that is integrated into the compositional process from the outset. Movement, depth, height, and the distribution of sounds thus appear as parameters that can be treated just as consciously during writing and arranging as pitch, rhythm, or timbre.

B-Format as a Compositional Storage Medium

The B-format was developed within the framework of Michael Gerzon’s Ambisonics theory and serves as the central signal format for encoding spatial audio data (Thornton, 2009). Its mathematical basis lies in the description of sound fields by means of spherical harmonics, through which a sound field around a reference point can be represented as a weighted sum of spatial components (Zotter & Frank, n.d.). The format functions as an intermediate representation between recording, processing, and playback: spatial information is first stored independently of any particular playback system and only then decoded for a given target setup, whether headphones, stereo, loudspeaker arrays, virtual reality applications, or 360-degree video (Ambisonics: File Formats, 2003). This separation of encoding and playback is compositionally significant because it allows spatial structures to be conceived first as abstract relations and only in a second step adapted to a concrete playback system.

In the narrower sense, the B-format refers to the four-channel first-order Ambisonics format consisting of the channels W, X, Y, and Z. W represents the omnidirectional pressure component of the sound field, whereas X, Y, and Z encode the directional components along the three spatial axes. In their weighting and relation to one another, they form the basis for the spatial localization of sonic events. In a broader sense, the term may also refer to higher-order Ambisonics formats, in which additional coefficients of the sound field are represented as separate audio channels, thereby allowing a higher spatial resolution. As a spatial frame of reference, the ICST Ambisonics plugins use a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system in which the X axis describes the left-right direction, the Y axis the front-back direction, and the Z axis the vertical dimension. Within this frame, positions, movements, and spatial relations can be described unambiguously and reproduced consistently (IEEE 3D Body Processing, n.d.).

Alongside the signal format itself, there are different conventions of normalization and channel ordering, in particular FuMa, SN3D, and N3D, which regulate transferability between different systems. In current production contexts and for data exchange, SN3D/ACN has become the most widely adopted standard; the ICST Ambisonics plugins have also used this format since Version 2 (Schweizerweb, 2023; Ambisonic Data Exchange Formats, n.d.; VRTONUNG, 2024).

Spatial Parameters as Compositional Categories

On the basis of the B-format, spatial properties can be understood as independent compositional parameters. Relations of direction, presence, trajectories of movement, and spatial density thus appear not as secondary qualities of playback, but as constitutive elements of musical form. The following sections define the individual parameters and connect each of them to its psychoacoustic foundation, since the compositional effectiveness of a parameter depends on its perceptual grounding.

Azimuth. Azimuth describes the horizontal position of a sound source in space. Compositionally, it makes it possible to organize lateral separation, approach, crossing, and contrary motion. Sounds can thus be not only localized, but also structured in their relation to one another; azimuth becomes a means of spatial voice-leading. Its psychoacoustic basis lies in the interaction of interaural time differences (Interaural Time Difference, ITD) and interaural level differences (Interaural Level Difference, ILD). ITD refers to the difference in arrival time between the two ears and is the dominant localization cue especially at low frequencies; ILD describes the level difference caused by the acoustic shadow of the head and is particularly effective at higher frequencies (Blauert, 1997). Together, these cues form the basis of lateral localization.

Elevation. Elevation refers to the vertical position of a sonic event and extends the horizontal organization of space by an independent layer. Compositionally, height can serve to separate sound layers, establish hierarchies, or produce specific spatial qualities. Particularly in Ambisonics, the vertical dimension becomes an autonomous formal parameter. Psychoacoustically, the perception of height depends on information that interaural differences alone cannot provide. Here, individually shaped Head-Related Transfer Functions (HRTF) are decisive: the head, torso, and especially the pinnae filter incoming sound in a direction-dependent manner and create characteristic spectral signatures that are crucial for the perception of elevation and for resolving front-back ambiguity (Blauert, 1997; Begault, 1994). From a compositional perspective, it is important that strong spectral interventions or unsuitable playback conditions can weaken these cues and thereby impair vertical differentiation.

Distance. Distance determines the degree of nearness and farness of a sound source. It does not operate only as a matter of level, but also affects presence, depth stratification, and perspectival perception: near sounds may be experienced as immediate, detailed, and bodily, whereas distant sounds may appear withdrawn or atmospheric. Distance is therefore both an acoustic and a dramaturgical parameter. Psychoacoustically, distance perception relies on several factors. The physical drop in intensity according to the inverse-square law is the most obvious cue, but in musical contexts it is often insufficient because levels may vary for expressive reasons. More effective is the ratio of direct sound to diffuse room energy: a high proportion of direct sound is perceived as proximity, while a dominant proportion of reflections and reverberation suggests distance (Rumsey, 2001). This is complemented by air absorption, which attenuates high frequencies with increasing distance, and by the growing blur of lateral differences at large distances (Begault, 1994).

Diffusion. Diffusion refers to the degree of spatial extension or focus of a sound. A sound may appear punctual and precise, hover as a diffuse cloud in space, or spread broadly and without clear boundaries. Compositionally, diffusion concerns the relation between focus and blur, between clear localizability and spatial dissolution; it is particularly useful for shaping transitions between object character and field character. Psychoacoustically, this parameter corresponds to the concept of Listener Envelopment (LEV): diffusely arriving reverberation produces the sensation of immersion and spatial embedding, whereas early reflections influence the perceived source width (Apparent Source Width, ASW) (Morimoto & Maekawa, 1988; see also additional literature on room-acoustic perception). Reverberation time and the spectral character of reverberation likewise shape spatial identity and can therefore be treated as independent compositional features.

Movement as Form. Spatial parameters rarely operate in isolation; they usually act in combination. Movements arise through temporal changes in azimuth, elevation, distance, and diffusion. In this way, movement itself becomes a form-generating process: approach, rotation, ascent, flight, or circling can assume motivic, dramaturgical, and structural functions. Spatial form thus emerges from the temporal organization of several interwoven parameters. Psychoacoustically, the perception of movement results from the coordinated variation of several cues, in particular ITD, ILD, HRTF patterns, as well as distance and reverberation relations (Blauert, 1997). In the case of rapid movement, the Doppler effect may additionally function as a cue. In binaural playback, the listener’s own head movement contributes to the stabilization of localization; if this reference is absent, for instance in static binaural playback without head tracking, externalization may become unstable (Begault, 1994).

Synthesis: Space as a System of Compositional Relations

The preceding analysis shows that azimuth, elevation, distance, diffusion, and movement are not to be understood as additive individual parameters, but as interdependent dimensions of a system. Their compositional effectiveness unfolds through coordinated shaping and mutual interweaving: a convincingly designed 3D sound image presupposes that several perceptual cues are treated simultaneously and consistently. Against this background, space appears not as a neutral site of playback, but as a structured field of relations whose parameters contribute to the formation of the work. The B-format provides the technical condition for fixing, transforming, and transferring these relations independently of any particular playback system, and thus for enabling precisely that compositional treatment of space which served as the point of departure in the concept of Spatial Counterpoint.
